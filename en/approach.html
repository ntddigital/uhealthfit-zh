Our approach to herbs

Chinese herbalism, explained without the mystique

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is one of the world's oldest continuously documented herbal traditions. You don't have to adopt its whole philosophy to benefit from what it does well — combining plants thoughtfully, for steady everyday support. Here's how we use it, and where we draw the lines.

The short version

What TCM is — and what it isn't

It is

  • A framework that looks for patterns across the whole body, not just one complaint
  • A large library of herb combinations, refined through centuries of recorded use
  • A daily, habit-based way of looking after yourself

It isn't

  • A replacement for diagnosis, prescriptions or professional care
  • A reason to stop any medication
  • Proof that something works the same way for every person
Five ideas worth borrowing

What we take from the tradition

01

Formulas over single ingredients

Classical formulas give each herb a job — a lead herb, supporting herbs, herbs that balance the blend and one that “guides” it (jun-chen-zuo-shi). It's closer to a well-built recipe than to a single-ingredient pill, and it's the main thing our formulas borrow.

02

Support, don't override

The tradition prefers nudging the body back toward its own rhythm over forcing a result. That's why our sleep formula is melatonin-free, our digestive formula skips senna, and our energy formula has no caffeine.†

03

Connections across the body

Classical texts link eye comfort to overall nourishment, teeth to the body's “deeper reserves”, skin to the breath. Treat these as the tradition's maps, not anatomy lessons — they explain why our formulas are built the way they are.

04

Steady and gentle

Tonic herbs are traditionally taken daily, in food-like amounts, over time. Our directions follow suit: a few capsules with meals, not a dramatic short course.

05

Food and herbs overlap

Goji, Chinese yam, chrysanthemum, lily bulb and citrus peel are everyday foods in Chinese kitchens. Much of herbalism starts where cooking ends.

A short glossary

Six words you'll see on our pages

  • QiThe tradition's word for functional energy — roughly, “how well things are running”.
  • Yin and yangComplementary qualities to keep in balance: cooling and moistening (yin) versus warming and activating (yang).
  • Tonic herbAn herb traditionally taken over time to nourish, such as goji, astragalus or Chinese yam.
  • AdaptogenA modern Western term for herbs that support the body's response to everyday stress — eleuthero, schisandra and American ginseng among them.
  • FormulaA combination of herbs with assigned roles. Most traditional preparations are formulas, not single herbs.
  • GaoA traditional concentrated herbal paste. EnerBalance is a gao, sealed into capsules.
Where we draw the lines

Tradition is the starting point. Standards are the rule.

  • Made where we can check itCapsules, creams and serum are made in the USA (with imported botanicals) in cGMP facilities; the eye patches are made in Japan.
  • Tested, not assumedFinished products are third-party tested for purity — a fair question to ask of any imported botanical.
  • Every ingredient on the labelLatin names on our supplement facts. Like most herbal formulas, the label shows the blend's total per serving rather than each herb's amount; we say so plainly.
  • Honest about interactionsHerbs such as salvia, dong quai and schisandra can interact with blood thinners. Each product page lists who should skip it or ask a doctor first.
  • No disease claims, no timelinesWe don't say our products treat anything, and we don't promise results by a date. Individual experiences vary.
  • No testimonialsWe'd rather show you the label than a stranger's story.
