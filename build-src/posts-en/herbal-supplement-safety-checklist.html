If you're curious about Chinese herbal supplements but a little wary, that's a sensible place to start. The questions that come up are good ones. Is this regulated? What's actually in the capsule? Was it tested? Will it get along with the medication I already take?

Maybe you've already done some homework: read a label or two, asked a pharmacist, looked up an unfamiliar ingredient. That instinct is right. Vetting a herbal formula isn't very different from vetting a multivitamin or a protein powder; there are just a few extra questions worth asking.

Here's a practical checklist you can use on any brand, including ours.

What's going on, in plain terms

In the US, herbal supplements are regulated as dietary supplements, a category of food rather than drugs. That means the FDA doesn't approve them before they're sold. Manufacturers are responsible for safety and accurate labeling, facilities are expected to follow current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP), and label claims are limited to how a product supports normal structure and function, not what it does for a disease.

One phrase worth decoding: "FDA-registered facility." Registration means the FDA knows the facility exists and can inspect it. That's useful, but it is not FDA approval, and a careful brand won't blur the two.

The Chinese herbal tradition adds one more layer. Classical products are usually formulas, not single herbs, built with defined roles known as jun-chen-zuo-shi: lead, support, balance and guide. So vetting a formula means looking at the whole ingredient list, not hunting for one star ingredient.

What to look for

A full ingredient list, with Latin names and an honest blend

Every botanical should be named, ideally with its Latin name and the part of the plant used. Common names can be loose: "ginseng" might mean Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng) or American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius), and the tradition uses them differently. Latin names remove the guesswork.

Many herbal formulas are labeled as a blend, with a total amount per serving instead of an amount for each herb. That's a common, legal format and not a red flag on its own. What matters is honesty: every ingredient named, the total blend amount stated, and a brand willing to answer questions. A blend name that tells you nothing, wrapped around ingredients you can't identify, is a different story.

Where it's made, and what the testing covers

Look for a stated country of manufacture, cGMP facilities and third-party testing. Then ask one more question: what does the testing cover? For any imported botanical, contaminants such as heavy metals, pesticide residues and microbes are fair things to ask about. That isn't a reason to fear imported herbs. It's simply what quality control looks like for agricultural products, and a good brand will tell you what it tests for.

Interactions, and who should skip it

Herbs are active plants, so interactions are real. Two categories to ask about first are blood thinners, since several traditional herbs, including salvia root and dong quai, are flagged here, and sedatives, where calming herbs can add up. Bring the bottle to your doctor or pharmacist if you take any prescription medication, and tell your surgeon about every supplement before a procedure.

Check, too, who the label says should skip it. Pregnancy and nursing, and anyone under 18, are standard exclusions for most herbal formulas. If a product has no "who should skip this" information at all, count that as missing information.

Label language that stays realistic

Supplement labels in the US should carry the standard FDA statement: a short note that the claims haven't been evaluated by the FDA and that the product isn't intended for any disease. Claims should describe support for normal function, in measured words, and stay away from disease names.

Red flags worth walking away from:

  • Big promises: a product that says it will make a health condition go away, works for everything, or lets you stop a prescription.
  • Before-and-after photos.
  • Timelines: a set number of days until you'll look or feel different.
  • Pressure tactics, such as countdown timers and "ends tonight" pricing.
  • A white coat doing the persuading instead of the label.
  • Mystery framing: talk of hidden ancient knowledge, paired with very little actual information.

Honest expectations

A well-vetted supplement is still a supplement. It isn't a medicine and doesn't replace your doctor's advice. Herbal formulas are built for steady daily use; individual experiences vary, and a clean label doesn't change that.

Skip herbal formulas during pregnancy or nursing, and for anyone under 18, unless a doctor says otherwise. If you take prescription medication, especially blood thinners or sedatives, talk to your doctor first. And if anything unexpected happens after you start something new, stop taking it and call your doctor.

FAQ

Are Chinese herbal supplements regulated in the US?

Yes, as dietary supplements. They don't need FDA approval before sale, but manufacturers must follow cGMP, label accurately and keep claims to structure and function. That's why checking how a brand makes and tests its products matters.

Is it rude to ask a brand about heavy metal testing?

Not at all. It's a fair, ordinary question for any imported botanical, and a brand that takes quality seriously should be glad to answer it.

Is a "proprietary blend" always a bad sign?

No. A blend lists the ingredients together with a total amount, which is common for herbal formulas. Look for every ingredient named and a clear total per serving, and if something is unclear, ask.
