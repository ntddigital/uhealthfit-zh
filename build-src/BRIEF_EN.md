# UHealthFit × WiseQuest — English pages: copy brief (for writers and writing agents)

The English pages live under `/en/` on us.uhealthfit.com. They are **not translations** of the Chinese pages. They are written for mainstream, English-speaking American adults (roughly 30–60) who meet Chinese herbal products with a mix of curiosity and skepticism. Every "Buy" button sends them to the matching English product page on **uhealthfit.com**, so every fact we state must match that page (facts table below).

## 1. Who is reading, and what they already believe

Assume the reader:
- Knows the supplement aisle: melatonin, magnesium, fiber (psyllium), probiotics, lutein/zeaxanthin (AREDS2-style eye vitamins), ashwagandha and "adaptogens", collagen, turmeric, chamomile and valerian tea.
- Knows skincare from Target/Sephora and K-beauty: cica (centella), ceramides, hyaluronic acid, peptides, retinol, colloidal oatmeal (the Aveeno ingredient), under-eye hydrogel patches as a self-care ritual.
- Has heard of acupuncture, ginseng, goji berries, green tea, maybe "qi" — but finds TCM jargon ("the liver opens into the eyes", "dampness", "yin deficiency") vague or mystical.
- Is **skeptical** in specific ways: "Is this regulated?" "Is it contaminated?" (heavy metals in imported herbs is a known worry) "Will it interact with my medication?" "Is this a proprietary-blend gimmick?" "Is this just a placebo?" "Why is it so expensive?"
- Trusts: US manufacturing, cGMP, third-party testing, full ingredient lists with Latin names, clear dosing, plain disclaimers, honest "who should skip this", and brands that don't overclaim.
- Distrusts: "ancient secret", "miracle", exoticism, doctor-endorsement theater, before/after photos, countdown timers.

## 2. How we talk about Chinese herbalism (the core of the English voice)

- **Lead with the everyday situation, not the theory.** Screen-heavy workdays, the 3 p.m. slump, lying awake replaying the day, bloating after a long desk day, reactive skin when the weather turns, gum care after 40. Use American context (Zoom calls, commutes, coffee runs, the second monitor, dentist visits twice a year).
- **Then introduce the tradition as a framework, not a fact about anatomy.** "In the Chinese herbal tradition, eye comfort is linked to the body's overall nourishment — which is why classical eye formulas pair eye-focused herbs like chrysanthemum with tonic herbs like goji." Always "in the tradition", "classical formulas", "traditionally used", never "your liver is causing…".
- **Formulas, not single ingredients.** Explain that TCM works in combinations with roles (the *jun-chen-zuo-shi* / "lead, support, balance, guide" structure) — comparable to how a good recipe or a well-built supplement stack works. This is the honest difference from single-ingredient products.
- **Bridge to what they already know.** Goji is one of the richest food sources of zeaxanthin; chrysanthemum tea is a café staple in Asia; American ginseng is native to North America and has been exported to China since the 1700s; centella is the "cica" in K-beauty; poria is a fungus (the functional-mushroom crowd will relate); eleuthero and schisandra are classic adaptogens; hemp seed is a familiar superfood.
- **Translate jargon on first use, once.** "Qi — the tradition's word for functional energy, roughly 'how well things are running'." Don't stack jargon.
- **Respect both systems.** "We respect modern medicine and value traditional herbal knowledge." Never "Western medicine only treats symptoms." Never mock or disparage other products or categories — explain what each one is built to do and what it isn't built to do.
- **No exoticism.** No "ancient secrets of the East", no "mystical", no dragons, no "Oriental". Plain, warm, specific.
- **Honest about evidence.** Traditional use ≠ clinical proof. Say "traditionally used for", "studied in modern research for" only where it's true and general (e.g., goji/zeaxanthin; centella's popularity in skincare). Never cite numbers, studies, or percentages.

## 3. Voice

Calm, plain-spoken, evidence-respecting, a little warm. Think "a knowledgeable friend who trained in both systems", not an influencer. "We" for the brand, "you" for the reader. Short paragraphs (2–4 sentences). Concrete over abstract. American spelling. No exclamation marks in body copy. No emoji.

Recurring ideas (use, don't overuse):
- "Support, don't override." / "Not forcing — supporting."
- "Built for steady, everyday use."
- "Know exactly what you're taking." (full ingredient lists)
- "Herbs are support, not a substitute for your doctor, dentist or eye exam."
- Brand line: "Your body has been working hard for you. Now it's our turn to look after it."

## 4. Compliance red lines (FTC / FDA-DSHEA / Google Ads health / Meta health & personal-attributes) — any breach = rewrite

1. **No disease or diagnosis names**, ever: dry eye (disease), eye strain syndrome/computer vision syndrome, macular degeneration, cataracts, glaucoma, myopia, insomnia, sleep disorder, anxiety, depression, constipation, IBS, diarrhea (except in a "stop use and see a doctor if…" warning), gingivitis, periodontitis, receding gums, bone loss, osteoporosis, acne (use "oily & combination skin"), eczema, rosacea, psoriasis, dermatitis, inflammation. Use situations instead: "eyes that feel tired after long screen days", "nights when it's hard to switch off", "that heavy, bloated feeling after a long desk day", "skin that feels reactive when the seasons change", "everyday gum care".
2. **Supplements: structure/function verbs only** — supports, helps maintain, nourishes (in a traditional sense), promotes a sense of. **Cosmetics (eye patches, creams, serum): appearance and feel only** — hydrates, cools, refreshes, soothes the look of, comforts, conditions, "skin looks/feels…". **Never**: treat, cure, heal, prevent, repair (health), reverse, eliminate, fight, relieve, reduce [symptom], anti-inflammatory, detox, cleanse, boost immunity, anti-aging, wrinkle removal, "younger".
3. **Never state or imply the reader's health condition in second person.** ✗ "Your eyes are dry." ✗ "Struggling with insomnia?" ✓ "If your workday runs on screens…" ✓ "If you'd rather skip melatonin…"
4. **No before/after, no guarantees, no timelines** ("in 2 weeks", "4–8 weeks", "fast-acting"). Say "Herbal formulas are built for steady daily use; individual experiences vary."
5. **Never imply replacing medicine or professional care.** Always fine to say: "This isn't a medicine and doesn't replace your doctor's advice. If you take prescription medication — especially blood thinners — or are pregnant or nursing, talk to your doctor first."
6. **Banned words:** miracle, cure, guaranteed, 100%, best, #1, only, clinically proven, doctor-recommended, secret, detox, anti-aging, younger, flush, melt.
7. **No unverified numbers:** no repurchase rates, user counts, allergy rates, review counts, star ratings, study results.
8. **No testimonials or endorsements**, invented or real: no customer quotes, no named doctors/optometrists/influencers (do not mention Dr. Lonn, Jeff Garden, Tenne Fujimoto, or any person besides Dr. Huang), no invented quotes from Dr. Huang.
9. **TCM concepts** only as tradition: "in the Chinese herbal tradition…", "classical formulas…", "traditionally used to…". Never "qi deficiency causes your fatigue".
10. **Specific product claims we do not make** (even though some appear elsewhere): de-puffing, dark circles, "repairs the orbital area", bone density / alveolar bone, receding gums, itch relief, steroid comparisons, "clears breakouts", "lifts" (say "firmer-looking"), "clinical", unretouched case results.
11. Eye patches: if you mention watering, use only: "The cooling ingredients can make some people's eyes water a little. If you feel stinging or irritation, remove the patches and rinse with water."
12. Each supplement structure/function claim in product copy carries a dagger (†); the site footer holds the FDA statement. In guides, the standard disclaimer is added by the template — don't write one.

## 6. Guide format (files in `build-src/posts-en/<slug>.html`)

First line(s): JSON meta, then `---END-META---`, then an HTML fragment.
```
{"slug":"...","title":"(≤ 70 chars, includes the search phrase)","description":"(meta description, 140–160 chars)","excerpt":"(card summary, 18–30 words)","reading_min":6,"collection":"eye","primary_product":"eye-patches","keywords":["...","..."]}
---END-META---
<p>...</p>
```
`collection` is one of `eye, dental, vitality, sleep, skin, digestive, general`.

Allowed tags only: `<h2> <h3> <p> <ul> <ol> <li> <blockquote> <table> <thead> <tbody> <tr> <th> <td> <strong> <em> <a>`.

Length: 1,000–1,400 words. Structure:
1. Open (2–3 short paragraphs): the reader's situation in their own words; acknowledge what they've already tried (it wasn't wrong — it just does a different job); name what actually matters when choosing.
2. `<h2>` What's going on, in plain terms — the everyday explanation, then one short paragraph on how the Chinese herbal tradition frames the same thing.
3. `<h2>` The common options, side by side — a `<table>` (Option | What it's built to do | What it isn't built to do | When it fits). Respectful, no disparaging.
4. `<h2>` What to look for — 4 `<h3>` checks (e.g., full ingredient list with Latin names, where it's made and tested, interactions and who should skip it, whether it fits a routine you'll actually keep).
5. `<h2>` Where WiseQuest fits — 1–2 products from the facts table, how to use them, how to start small (e.g., the $6 patch trial).
6. `<h2>` Honest expectations — no timelines; who should skip it; when to see a doctor/dentist/eye doctor.
7. `<h2>` FAQ — 3 `<h3>` questions with short answers.
8. Close: one warm paragraph + one text link to the product page or the quiz.
